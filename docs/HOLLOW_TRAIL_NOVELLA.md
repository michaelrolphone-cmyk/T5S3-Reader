# Hollow Trail

*A novella*

## I. The Backward Stitch

The first light appeared where there ought to have been a hill.

I was washing a cup at the kitchen window. The water had gone cold. Beyond the glass, the garden wall ended in a whiteness so thick that the last apple tree seemed to stand alone at the edge of the world. For three nights the fog had kept the hills from us. That evening it reached the wall.

Through a crescent I had wiped in the greasy glass, the rake and bucket stood sharp against the wall. Beyond them the apple branches faded into fog. My reflection occupied the missing country, one hand still holding the cup.

Her cup was on the shelf behind me. It had a chip beside the handle that she turned away from her mouth. I had kept washing it although no one used it. Now it stood among the other cups with its handle pointing into an empty space.

The light came again.

A short, pale opening in the white. Then another. Then a third.

I put the cup down without rinsing it.

A soap bubble slid down the cup and spread beneath it. My other hand closed on the sill. I had not yet turned to fetch my coat. In the glass, my face had lifted.

My sister had taught me the signal one summer when the grass behind our house stood above my head. She would walk out into it with a pocket mirror and leave me watching from the upstairs window. Three flashes meant follow. I was to come by the ditch, where the grass lay down, and keep low until I reached her. We were hiding from no one. That was part of the pleasure: the precautions, the private importance of arriving unseen, her hand closing round my wrist when I stumbled into the little room she had flattened in the grass.

Inside our flattened room the grass was green to its roots; seed heads trembled above us. She pulled long stems across the entrance. Sometimes she looked toward the house, eyes narrowed, then bent over a beetle or a split seed pod with such absorption that I forgot she had been watching.

That summer she wore her hair in a loose braid. Fine strands escaped at her temples and stuck to the sweat there. I remember trying to brush one away. She caught my hand before it reached her face, kissed the knuckles without looking, and put it back in my lap.

Once she gave the wrong signal. Two short flashes and one held long enough to hurt my eyes.

I came anyway.

She was furious. I remember that more clearly than the game. Her fingers pressed into my arms; a grass seed clung to the wet corner of her mouth.

“That one means stay back.”

“You said it meant someone was watching.”

“Yes.”

“I was watching.”

She looked over my shoulder then. I turned to see what she saw, but there was only the upstairs window, an oblong of darkness under the eaves. When I looked back she was laughing, and the fright had become something I had misunderstood.

At the kitchen window I waited for the long flash.

Nothing came. The fog closed. Water ran from my hands into my sleeves.

She had been gone eleven days.

I did not leave that night. I want to put that down plainly. There was darkness to consider, and the fog, and the fact that a light seen through a wet window might be anything. I found enough reasons to stay until morning. Then I spent the night dressed in a chair, furious with each small domestic sound that suggested life could continue in the house without her.

Before dawn I took bread, a knife, dry stockings, the little lamp from the pantry and a notebook whose first pages she had used for household accounts. I tore those pages out. Later I would regret it. At the time I wanted a clean place in which to find her.

The cup remained beside the sink, cloudy with soap.

My coat caught on the latch. I turned angrily, then stood with a thread stretched between cloth and iron. Behind me, the house held the flat light of dawn. Her slippers lay beneath the stove, one across the other.

I freed the thread. Before closing the door, I pushed my chair under the table.

At the edge of the forest, the road loosened into roots.

Behind me, garden walls dwindled into boundary stones, then a ditch under alders. Bronze leaves overlapped on the water. At the last bend a white chimney still showed above an orchard; another few steps lowered it behind the bank.

The forest rose in long humps of rooted earth, with wet leaves whole in the hollows and ground to a black skin on the ridges. Russet bracken crowded the boulders. Above, a young tree grew through the opening left by a broken oak limb, its small leaves bright against the old crown. Mist passed through the gap and the daylight wandered.

Moss crossed from stone to bark. A fallen trunk had become a strip of ground, its hollow filled with shoots. The exposed wood crumbled into red-brown cubes when my boot brushed it. After that I stepped around it as carefully as the living roots.

Rain had carried the fine soil away, leaving ridges of pale wood between pockets of dark water. Each step needed looking at. My sister would have gone through without slowing, knowing where to set her feet by some attention I had never learned to see her give.

The trees were larger under the fog. Their upper limbs disappeared, and without crowns to measure them against they seemed capable of rising indefinitely. Close to the path, trunks stood black and wet, their bark folded into deep seams. Beyond them were other trunks, gray enough to be mistaken for spaces between trees. Beyond those, a long swell of hillside moved in and out of the whiteness.

An immense trunk hid the path. Two steps later the view returned, rearranged: a gray stem stood where I had expected open ground. I kept one hand out, measuring the way by what I could touch.

I kept finding shapes that might have been her.

A hooked branch carried a torn strip of cloth. I had almost passed it before I saw the hem.

She stitched badly when she was tired. One neat run, then a little backward bite with the needle, as though she had changed her mind about which way the fabric ought to go. This strip had two of them. I held it against my cheek before I thought to smell it. Wet wool, bark, the mineral cold of the morning. None of the warm things I had expected.

The cloth had been pushed into a cut in the tree.

A filament held the cloth to the bark. I loosened it separately, then smoothed the wool over my hand as if she might ask what I had done to it. Beneath it lay an older cut, rounded and grown over, where a wedge of bark had been removed facing the road.

Inside the folded hem was a paper narrow enough to roll between two fingers.

*I have left the route where you can reach it. Do not mistake a path I used for a path I chose.*

Below that she had drawn a small socket with its mouth turned toward the road. Her pen had dug through the paper at the corner.

Someone had cut a date into the bark. It was earlier than the morning she left our house. Below the date, in smaller letters, were the words:

*She knew which tree.*

I read them with my thumb resting on her uneven stitches.

Leaves began falling while I stood there. The branches above me were almost still, but farther into the wood a gust had found the canopy. The leaves came turning out of the gray, dark on one side and silver on the other, and caught in the roots at my feet. For a moment the whole forest seemed occupied by small things changing their minds.

I put the paper in the notebook. I left the cloth where it was.

There might be someone behind me who needed it.

The thought had scarcely formed before I looked back.

The path had already become difficult to distinguish. A black trunk blocked the place where I had entered; beyond it two others made a narrow, empty doorway. I listened. There was a soft ticking of water dropping from one leaf to another, and a low sound, intermittent, that might have been a branch rubbing against its neighbour.

I went on.

Past a hollow filled with brown water, a dead tree leaned against the bank. Its roots had been exposed and sawn partway through. The slope beyond it was too loose to climb. I put my shoulder to the trunk, felt the cold come through my coat, and pushed.

At first nothing happened. My feet slid in the leaf mould.

I braced my heel against a root. Bark flattened under my shoulder and spilled down my sleeve. Each effort brought my cheek against the wet trunk; each pause left me slipping backward.

The dead tree held its angle. A spider’s thread between two splinters remained unbroken. I tried higher, where the wood gave a little beneath the bark. My sister had once laughed at me for trying to move a table without taking anything off it. I heard her laugh now with such accuracy that I stopped and turned my head.

The forest was empty.

I went back to the trunk. This time I stayed against it after my arms began to shake. A root snapped beneath the soil. There was a deep, unwilling movement, then another. The tree inclined slowly, almost courteously, before its own weight took it.

The branches crossed the pale opening, slowly enough to count, then too quickly. The trunk slid from beneath my shoulder. I stumbled after it, arms raised, as a last root tore free trailing earth. The tree struck across the hollow with a crack that came back thinner from far away.

For a long time afterward I heard small things settling.

The mill stood in a dip between hills.

A stream widened above the mill into a leaf-covered pool. Clay showed where the bank had slumped; trickles carried silt across the reflection of the roof.

The building leaned into the slope. Fern and ivy hid its uphill stone wall; downhill, timber boards stood black at the foot and gray beneath the eaves. The roof sagged above the wheel. Saplings in the gutter cast shadows across a whole window. Beyond the hollow, the onward path climbed toward a strip of pale sky.

The mill’s wheel was still, half hidden beneath a skin of leaves. Water escaped around the boards in threads so fine they looked like cracks in glass. A doorway under the eaves had been barred from within.

I entered by a broken shutter and found the register on a sloping desk.

The desk was hollowed where a hand had rested. Above it, a broken window cast its grid across the floor. In the beam reaching the register, thick strokes of ink shone; the rest of the writing sank into the page.

I drew the desk toward the light. One leg left a bright scrape through the dust. It was the first new mark in the room.

Seven names occupied a ruled column. Six had been written beneath my sister’s. Beside each was a square. Some squares were filled; others had been scratched at until the paper had thinned. The heading had been scraped away altogether.

One hand was small, upright and careful. The other was hers.

*Record every admission before opening the crossing,* the careful hand said. *She supplied the names without asking the people to speak.*

My sister had crossed out *admission* so hard that I could feel the damage on the back of the sheet.

*They were too cold to answer you. You knew that. I wrote for them.*

I could see her at that desk. Not a vision; a memory finding furniture to inhabit. She bent over work with one shoulder higher than the other, her hair falling forward until she became irritated and pushed it behind her ear with the back of her wrist. When she was angry, she held the pen low enough to stain her fingers.

I had made her angry on the day she left.

“You always find someone who needs you more,” I had said.

She stood in our doorway with her scarf half wound, one end hanging below her coat. There were things I could have done: finish winding it for her, lift the bag she was trying to shoulder, ask who waited beyond the road. Instead I watched her make room in the bag for a second loaf.

“Come, then,” she said.

I thought she meant it to punish me.

In the mill, the paper trembled against my thumb.

I steadied the page with my other hand, covering the six names beneath hers. Then I moved it away.

Under the argument, the careful writer had added a final line.

*I kept the last square open.*

He signed himself *the keeper*.

There was no name.

Beyond the mill the forest lifted. Trees thinned along the edge of a ravine, and the light grew broad and colourless. I crossed by a rope knotted around a living branch. The rope was coarse and swollen with damp; its twists pressed a pattern into my palms. When I put my weight on it, the branch bowed toward the emptiness.

Halfway across I looked down.

There was water far below, or perhaps a strip of sky lying between stones. My stomach seemed to remain at the bank while the rest of me moved. I shut my eyes, which made it worse. The rope slid an inch through my hands. I opened them to the world again and caught the opposite root with my heel.

On the far side I lay with my face in the earth until the hammering in my chest became separate from the movement of the branch.

From there I could see the clearing.

Beyond the black border of ferns, hills lay behind hills. Near trees had separate limbs; farther crowns joined along a ridge; on the last slope, earth and trees were one gray shape. The path crossed the clearing like a dark stitch through empty cloth.

I stared into a white opening between the farthest ridges, waiting for something to emerge because there was room for it. As I crossed, my shadow thinned ahead of me and disappeared.

A wooden bell hung from a branch at its edge. A nail suspended inside it struck the wood when I touched it, making a small, homely sound.

Inside was another shaped socket, and a loop of paper.

*Three short flashes: follow. Two short and one long: someone is watching. You remember.*

Beneath her words, the keeper had written:

*She gave me both patterns before I asked.*

I touched the nail again. The same small sound.

I had thought the warning belonged to us.

At the far end of the clearing, three wooden shafts held the gate against its frame. The rubbings in the papers showed how they should fit. Turning one moved its neighbour; the last disturbed the first. I worked until my hands were black with wet iron and old grease. Twice I thought I had finished, and twice the frame held.

When the shafts finally settled, the sound was no louder than someone putting down a cup.

The gate moved inward. Through it I saw the upper floors of the city rising from fog.

Trees continued down gullies between the first houses. Walls climbed among the roots; a drain emerged above a stone cornice; a fence vanished into thicket and returned beside a stair. Beyond these low roofs, towers stood through the fog at different depths, black nearby and almost white across the basin. The hills continued under the city, divided now into walls and window grids. The towers’ foundations remained hidden.

## II. Rooms Above the Fog

The city began overhead.

Its first buildings had sunk into white air up to their third or fourth floors. Rooflines overlapped like dark cards stood on edge, some close enough to touch, others reduced to smudges behind them. Aerials leaned from chimney stacks. Cables crossed between walls and disappeared before reaching whatever held the other end.

A broken balustrade stood against rows of windows, and beyond them a tower almost rubbed away by fog. No streets joined these buildings. Each roof seemed a separate island.

Close beside me, the corner of a wall interrupted half the view. Its mortar joints were thick with soot. I had to lean around it to see the ladder, and in doing so exposed myself to the open court. I drew back at once, though there was no one visible to draw back from.

I came out of the forest onto an abandoned service terrace. Water stood in the gutters. A ladder had been lowered from a roof above, its bottom rung resting in a puddle with such exactness that I thought someone must have set it down a moment before.

I called her name.

The word passed into the gap between buildings. I heard its shape come back without its warmth.

There was a locked door beside the ladder. The paint around the bolt had been rubbed smooth by many hands.

Above the latch, bare wood held the shape of a thumb. An old palm print on the panel was larger than mine. Beside it my sister had drawn a charcoal arrow. Another hand had crossed it out and drawn a sharper arrow along the same route.

The ladder flexed under me. By the time I reached the roof, the road had vanished.

Grit filled the gutter. Patches lifted along the roof; a broken nest clung to a chimney cage. Behind the stack, a shallow tank reflected the sky so brightly that it looked like an opening.

The next block retained the outline of a vanished house: a roof angle, beam holes, two pale rooms exposed high on its wall. Wallpaper survived beneath a ledge. Beyond it, roofs crowded into towers, their tops hard against the cloud while fog erased the floors below.

I followed the gutter. With each step the chimney hid a different building. A city could disappear behind a few feet of brick.

A schoolroom occupied the top floor. The roof had leaked into it. Rows of desks stood under a ceiling dark with damp, and a map lay flat beneath four pieces of brick. My sister had marked a path across the printed district, taking the roofs one by one.

*I have put the ladder where you will need it. When the stair door shuts, do not wait beneath it.*

The date preceded the closure written on a notice downstairs.

The keeper had circled both dates.

*Who told her which door would lock?*

My sister’s answer ran into the margin.

*There was no other way.*

I sat at a child’s desk. My knees did not fit beneath it. On the underside someone had carved half a name and abandoned it.

Rain had erased the blackboard above the height of a child’s reach. Cups on the sill held dead stems in hard soil. I put my bag on the floor and sat straight, feet together, among the narrow desks.

From this room every roof on her route could be seen. I followed the charcoal line with a finger. It approached the brightest windows, crossed the widest open spaces, and passed above the alleys without ever dropping into them.

The map had been folded so often along the route that its paper had broken there.

I told myself she had been trying to stay where someone could see her.

I put that sentence in the notebook. Then, because I was ashamed of how quickly I had written it, I left space underneath.

The roofs were easier to cross when I did not look ahead. At a distance each gap seemed too wide. Close up there would be a ledge below it, a rain pipe, a place where another person’s soles had worn the soot from the stone. Her preparations kept resolving the city into something I could survive.

That should have comforted me.

At the first high crossing, I had to stand on a crate to reach the parapet. The crate had been dragged there through standing water. Its runners left two clean tracks in the grit. I put my hands where hands had polished the coping and lifted myself into the wind.

Below me, the fog opened.

For perhaps a second I saw a street. A cart stood on its side beside a row of shuttered shops. A sheet of pale material had caught across the cart’s wheels. Water moved under it, lifting and dropping the fabric with a slow, continuous effort.

I saw no one.

Then the white air thickened and the street went away.

Across the gap, I found a fuse label tied to a railing. The wire beside it was blackened. A second, newer length had been taped along the first with strips cut from a flour sack.

The keeper’s writing was crowded at the bottom of the label.

*I disconnected the beacon after the first report. No further travellers should be called until we know where they go.*

My sister had written on the back.

*You disconnected the only light they could see. They disappeared from your windows because they went below them.*

There was more, but rain had taken it.

It began falling again as I turned the label over. A few large drops at first, dark spots on the white flour-sack tape. Then the sky lowered against the chimneys, and rain ran across the roof in long, hurried sheets.

I took shelter beneath a water tank. Its iron belly hung over me on legs thick with rust. Each drop that struck it made a hollow sound. A ladder rose through the frame and ended against a hatch secured from above.

Through the rain I could see a light in the next building.

Three flashes. A pause. Three again.

I was on my feet before the third had finished.

The distance between us was only a narrow court. I shouted and heard the rain take the sound apart. The light repeated. I waved the fuse label, absurdly, as though she might recognise a piece of paper at that distance.

Rain blinded me. I wiped my eyes with a sooty hand. Each flash lit the opposite sill and showed darkness behind it. I leaned until the railing pressed beneath my ribs, trying to separate someone inside from a reflected chimney.

My raised arm began to ache. I kept it raised. My sister had hated being waved at with things. “Use your hand,” she used to say. “I know your hand.”

I waved my hand.

The light gave three flashes.

The route to it took me up another ladder and along a parapet so narrow I had to keep one cheek against the wall. Rain ran from the coping into my collar. At a corner the wind lifted the back of my coat, and I pressed myself flat, mouth open against wet brick, waiting for it to leave me alone.

When I reached the window, I struck the glass with my palm.

No one moved inside.

The room was bare except for a chair and a lamp mounted beside a rotating wheel. A little metal tongue rose, held, fell; the light flashed. The wheel turned. The tongue rose again.

The wheel’s teeth shone through the dust. Oil hung beneath the axle in a bead that never fell. Each turn whitened the chair, laid light across the floor, and withdrew it.

My hand was still against the glass. In the next flash I saw the marks of my fingers spreading on its outer surface. I lowered the hand slowly, one fingertip after another.

I watched the whole sequence twice.

The chair faced the window. There was a hollow in its rush seat, and a coat hook above it with nothing on the hook. I opened the window and climbed in. The room smelled of dust warmed by electricity.

Beside the chair lay a watch log.

Her signature appeared seven times on two facing pages. I knew the downward stroke that broke near the bottom; I knew the small, impatient blot where she stopped to think. I had seen it on lists left beside our stove, on a note asking me to bring in the washing, on a letter she started and never sent.

Here the blot appeared twice in precisely the same place.

I laid the pages against the window. Outside, gray towers stood in the rain. Their lit windows shone through the paper, and for a moment the writing appeared to belong to the city itself.

Against the window, the blots fitted exactly. I moved one sheet aside and returned it; the same hook found the same gap. My breath dampened the paper. I kept holding it carefully, though I wanted it to come apart.

The keeper had left a sentence along the bottom.

*Ask who attended, not whose name appears.*

I did not want to give him that sentence. I wanted him to be the man who locked a door and put a generous act into a narrow column. I wanted his care with dates to be a kind of cowardice. It was inconvenient that he had noticed something I would have preferred to miss.

My sister had written elsewhere in the log:

*Nobody should keep the whole watch alone.*

I sat in the empty chair.

At home, during the winter our mother was ill, my sister would wake me for my turn at the bed. She always said the same thing: “You’ve only to sit there.” But she stayed in the doorway after I sat, waiting until I had picked up the cup or straightened the blanket. Sometimes I pretended to sleep through her knocking. I could recognise the moment she decided not to knock again. The silence after that decision was different from the silence before it.

The lamp flashed beside my head.

I stayed until my coat stopped dripping.

At each flash my open hands cast long shadows between my feet. Outside, the empty chair had frightened me. Sitting in it, I made the same pool of light look occupied.

When the rain weakened, the city became visible farther west.

A flooded street shone through the fog, then vanished beneath a bridge of rooms. Grass trembled from a cracked cornice. Westward, warehouses thinned into fenced yards and sidings. Beyond the drainage cuts, low bluffs retained the afternoon light.

Roofs stepped down through layers of gray, and beyond them a flat country spread under a lid of low cloud. Tall shapes stood there with bent necks. I thought at first they were trees stripped of all but one branch.

The relay cabinet at the end of the roof terrace had three contact banks. Inside, a scorched crossover carried the smell of burned cloth. The watch log showed how the central return fed the heaters below. The outer lines led toward the lamps.

I moved the contacts slowly, using the dry corner of my sleeve. The first arrangement made a sharp blue snap. I jerked back and waited for smoke. None came. I found the blackened path, bypassed it, and tried again.

A row of windows below me lit.

So did the signal behind my shoulder.

The lock on the service stair withdrew with a heavy click.

I had made a way out. I had also made the city call again.

As I went down the stairs I heard the little metal tongue lift, fall, lift.

For the first time, I was glad I could no longer see the light.

## III. The Eighth Place

The bent shapes were pumps.

They stood across the plain with their heads lowered, every one stopped at a different point in its labour. Some leaned almost to the earth; others had lifted their counterweights and held them there.

The nearest counterweight hung like a crescent, paint curling from its edge; its rod had worn a bright slot through the rust. Across the wind-folded plain, the machinery dwindled to dark gestures. I walked toward one pump until another rose between us. Grass grew through their pads. Nothing moved.

The service lamps were burning.

Their light scarcely showed in the afternoon, but at the mouth of each shed a faint yellow stain lay on the ground. It was enough to make the unlit spaces beside it seem inhabited. Twice I turned toward the corner of a building because I thought someone had moved out of the way.

The road ran between storage tanks.

The tanks stood on terraces cut into a basin. Water in the tyre ruts held narrow pieces of sky; washed-out culverts projected over gullies of flattened grass. Above the eastern rim, the city’s towers still showed, pale and thin. Northward, weather had hollowed the rock beneath harder caps. Low shrubs followed the damp seams.

Pipes crossed old foundations and fences abandoned inside newer fences. From the road the pumps seemed scattered; as I climbed, they fell into lines following buried connections. The light glanced hard off metal. Beneath one tank, a shadow looked deep enough to enter. Close up it was a shallow break in the concrete.

The tanks’ curved walls were streaked where rain had worked through the rust, and the puddles around their bases held a thin skin that gathered colours when the wind touched it. I smelled oil, cold iron, and something sweet gone bad beneath both.

I had not eaten since morning. The bread had flattened against the notebook in my bag. When I tore a piece off, a little rectangle of paper came with it, one of the household pages I had failed to remove completely. On it my sister had written *six candles* and then crossed out *six* and put *four*.

I ate standing beside a lamp.

Her ration tag was tied around its handle.

The string had been knotted so tightly that I needed the knife to loosen it. Seven allowances were listed, and hers had been crossed out. Three smaller entries below it had been increased.

Her number remained beneath the crossing-out. Tilting the tag brought it into the light; tilting it back buried it. All three increased portions had been ticked.

*Let them think there is enough,* she had written.

I pressed the heel of the bread into my mouth and could not swallow.

At home she cut thick slices for other people and thin ones for herself. It had annoyed me. There were times when her doing without filled a room more completely than someone else’s taking too much. Once, at supper, I had put my plate in front of her and taken hers away. She pushed it back without looking up.

“Don’t do that.”

“You do it.”

“I know what I’ve eaten.”

“So do I.”

We had let our food go cold between us.

Under the tag, folded against the lamp’s warm metal, was a requisition sheet. The keeper’s figures were exact. He had divided the fuel by hours of darkness and by the number of lamps, leaving a narrow margin that vanished halfway through the second page.

*The allotment was not hers to give,* he wrote. *She was told what would happen if the lamps went out. She agreed before I finished reading the conditions.*

There were marks beneath his sentence where another sheet had been written on top. I turned the paper toward the lamp. One word rose out of the indentations.

*Continue.*

A gust came across the plain, carrying grit. It struck the tanks with a dry whisper.

Dust followed the curve of a tank, escaped across the yard and hid the ladder’s lower rungs. I bent over the tag, sheltering it with my body. Grit filled the creases of my hand. I put the bread away.

From the service embankment, pipes surfaced and descended between the rows. The works had cut and raised the ground until I could scarcely separate them from the hills. Everything led somewhere underground.

I imagined her walking through here with six people behind her. Three children, two adults, an old man. I had their names in the register. I knew nothing else about them. In my mind the children changed height whenever I looked away. The old man borrowed the face of a neighbour who had died years before. One of the adults was always taller than my sister. I resented that invented height: why had someone with longer legs and stronger arms let her carry everything?

At the top of the embankment, I caught myself looking back to count them.

Only the pumps stood behind me.

The wind strengthened as I descended.

The road entered a cut just above my head. Roots hung from bands of clay and stone; scrub shadows shook across the walls. Beyond it the pump heads broke the skyline, their bases hidden by earthworks. Wind drove dust into the lower yard. At a broken culvert a round stone had rolled into the channel and lodged there. Someone had scraped away the bank beside it to make a foothold. The stone shifted when I stepped on it, just enough to send a soft pulse of panic up my leg. I crouched until it settled and crawled the last yard on my hands.

There was a stove in the next shed.

I entered expecting warmth, though no smoke came from the pipe. The room was narrow. Coats had hung along one wall; their outlines remained in the soot. Beneath them stood a bench worn smooth at the edge. A small pile of wood had been sorted by thickness under the stove, the thinnest pieces laid crosswise on top.

This order hurt me more than the crossed-out ration.

Someone had thought about the next cold person.

One shaved stick still held its curl of wood. The bench had been dragged toward the stove at one end, leaving two fresh marks beyond the older pair.

I took the seat at that end. There was no warmth left, but I put my palms toward the iron before lowering them to my knees.

I knelt to inspect the ash. It had formed a hard gray crust, but underneath, a black piece of wood retained the shape of something that had not quite finished burning. A potato skin lay beside the stove leg, curled into a small brown ear.

Behind a loose metal plate I found my sister’s note.

*Open the flue. The knocking frightened them, so I tied the loose pipe. Let them sleep.*

The binding was still there: wire wrapped in cloth, pulled hard around a section of pipe where it met the wall. I touched it. The cloth had become stiff with soot.

Two knocks sounded beneath the floor.

I did not move.

A third followed, longer, a shudder running through the metal under my fingers.

Soot fell across my cuff. In a dish on the stove, two rings spread through rainwater; the third tremor shook the whole surface. When it stilled, the doorway’s reflection returned.

I put my ear to the pipe.

The sound inside it was enormous and indistinct. Water somewhere, or wind passing a distant opening. My own breath grew loud enough to hide what I was listening for. I held it until my chest hurt.

Nothing.

“I’m here,” I said.

The words went into the stove and came back muffled.

I struck the pipe three times with the handle of my knife. The first blow was too light, and I did it again, more carefully.

For several minutes I waited for an answer.

Then I noticed that I was listening with my face turned toward the floor, exactly where she might have knelt. I moved away as if I had put my hand on something hot.

The keeper had written on the back of her note.

*The binding held. The knocks continued. She said the metal was cooling. Then she asked me to leave the room.*

Outside, grit scraped against the door.

I wanted to dislike him for staying long enough to hear what she had asked him not to hear. I wanted to ask why he had not opened the floor. I went around the room myself, pulling at boards with my fingers, but they were bolted to iron joists. A hatch under the bench had a recessed catch and no ring to lift it by.

I tried the knife until the tip bent.

When I stood, the light had changed. The dust outside was thinning. Through a crack in the wall I could see the side of a tank, pale now beneath the clearing sky, with one dark streak running down it from a seam.

The pipe did not knock again.

I left the stove door open.

Farther west, the ground dropped toward a distribution station. Empty cans stood under an awning. Their handles had been tied together so they would not blow away, and when I touched one the whole row moved with a soft clattering complaint.

The last can contained two lists rolled inside each other.

My sister had written on the outer one:

*Seven travelling now. Leave an eighth line. I cannot promise they will come, but I can leave room.*

On the inner list, the eighth line was already filled with the first letter of my name.

The date was earlier than her arrival at the station.

I sat on the low wall with the can between my feet.

I spread the lists across my knees: the empty line on the left, my initial on the right. I brought the sheets together, then parted them.

A gust lifted the blank list. I caught that one first. Beyond the tanks, the first rail embankment cut across the plain. It was close enough to seem reachable before dark. I stayed sitting.

When she left, she had said *come*. That was all. A word thrown back from the doorway while I was still trying to make her feel the weight of going.

I had treasured the belief that following her now was an act she had not asked of me. I could surprise her. I could arrive carrying food, or lift something she could no longer lift, or stand between her and whoever had made her write that first note. It had been a childish picture, but it was mine.

I took out the notebook and wrote down both dates. My hand was clumsy with cold. The first letter of my name, copied beside her list, looked strange to me. I crossed it out, then rubbed at the crossing until the paper began to pill.

Three vessels fed the station through connecting taps. She had marked them eight, five and three: eight measures to be divided equally between two watches, leaving the smallest vessel empty. I poured in the last light, each transfer stopping when a vessel filled or emptied. Twice I arrived back where I had begun. At last four measures stood in each of the larger vessels.

I released the feed. The service lamps steadied along the line of tanks.

Near my hand, the empty measure reflected the sky. A narrow, dark face looked up from its bottom, lengthened by the metal until I hardly recognised it.

I went toward the railway before the lamps became bright enough to guide me.

## IV. The Cancelled Train

I slept in a carriage with no wheels.

It stood on timber blocks beside the track, its underframe propped above a ditch. The door had swollen shut, but a broken window gave me room to climb inside. Rain had come through the roof and stained the seat opposite. I chose the dry bench, took off my boots, and put my feet under my coat.

The carriage smelled of old upholstery and mouse droppings.

Where the ceiling lining had fallen, ribs crossed above the luggage racks like an overturned hull. A strap hung in two split tongues. I set my boots beneath the bench, toes pointed into the aisle—the only part of me ready to go anywhere. Above them, a sign forbade passengers to lean out. Someone had underlined *passengers*.

I lay down expecting to hear the pipe again.

The signal gantry divided the fading sky. In the unbroken pane, my reflection lay sideways on the bench. I turned toward the seat back.

Instead I heard nothing.

The absence of sound kept me awake. In the forest there had always been a leaf, a root, water moving. In the city, rain and the relay. Even the oil field had pipes carrying distant activity beneath it. Here the tracks went away into the dark with nothing travelling on them. Once I thought a train was coming, a vibration growing through the bench. It was my own pulse against the wood.

Near dawn I dreamed my sister sat opposite me.

She was wearing her indoor dress and had put her feet on the seat, an untidiness she permitted herself only when we were alone. I tried to show her the papers, but each one turned out to be a household account. She looked at the columns and said I had left something out.

“What?”

She pointed to a blank place.

“You’ll remember.”

I woke with my hand extended across the carriage.

Morning showed me the locomotive on the parallel track.

Rails divided beneath a pale-grassed embankment and entered three cuttings. Weeds hid them in stretches; birches preserved the line of a lifted siding. Across the yard, a goods-shed door leaned from its guide. Through broken roof windows, rafters crossed strips of morning sky.

At the far cutting the hillside had fallen. Trees, roots and stone lay together in a heap larger than the station. Beneath a ragged overhang, the remaining rock showed oblique beds, pale at the face and dark along the seams.

The sky was clear. Without fog or rain to shorten the distances, I could see how long it would take someone to approach. I kept looking up the empty lines.

The locomotive had stopped facing a cutting whose far end was buried in fallen earth. Its wheels rested correctly on the rails, its couplings held, its lamp faced forward.

Connecting rods crossed the engine’s flank. A plant had reached the cab step: three leaves on an iron tread made for a boot. Behind it stood two carriages, intact as the locomotive. Nothing had been torn away. That completeness made the stillness difficult to accept.

I walked beside it, trailing my fingers along the cold steel. At the cab, a thin bird came out through the firebox and vanished between the rods. The surprise of that small living movement made me laugh. Then, before I could stop it, the laugh turned into a sound I would not have made if anyone had been there.

I stood under the cab step until it passed.

Inside the station, a ticket marked HOME had been pinned to a noticeboard.

The word was printed in the same ordinary type as the destination codes around it. Someone had punched the ticket. Its small missing circle gave me a view of the wood behind.

A fold crossed HOME. I smoothed it with my palm; it returned as soon as I let go.

*They told us the next train would not stop,* my sister had written. *We were not permitted to wait.*

The cancellation book lay open beneath it. The keeper’s account put the cancellation before the collapse.

*She asked me to cancel it before the snow. She said the passengers would stay if they believed a train was coming.*

Above the book hung a weather warning, its paper dry and stiff. The date was earlier still.

I stepped outside to look at the cutting.

A white scrape showed where the hillside had let go. Trees lay in the earth at odd angles, their roots turned outward. The rails entered the fall and did not come out. A length of telegraph wire descended into it like a line dropped through ice.

The cancellation had saved someone from waiting for a train that would never arrive.

I went back inside and read the ticket again.

The benches faced the tracks. There was room on them for all seven travellers, with a little space left at the end. I imagined them sitting there while she tried to persuade them to leave. The oldest man would have kept his hat on. One child would have asked the same question until an adult answered sharply. Someone would have offered to go and look, and she would have said there was no need, or there was no time, or she had already looked.

I could hear her making her voice patient.

I had disliked that voice when it was used on me. It made refusal feel like breaking something carefully held.

The ticket went into my notebook. I left the weather warning where it was.

West of the station, the route crossed a trestle. Its outer span had fallen into a ditch, leaving a supported section that ended in open air. A service rope hung from the gantry beyond it. Beneath the rails, sleepers rested on ballast and black earth; along the broken edge, their ends stuck out like books pulled unevenly from a shelf.

I had to climb down to the bracing and move across with the track above my head. Water trembled on each bolt. The supports were thick enough that I could trust them, but the spaces between were not small enough to forget.

I felt each joint before trusting it. Sleepers barred the sky above; broken timbers lay below. I followed the scraped knuckle of my leading hand into the gap. Behind me, pale palm prints diminished along the wet beam.

Halfway along I found a strip of wool wound around a splintered brace.

This time I did not lift it to my face.

I tested the next footing first.

At the signal cabin, a map had been corrected in her hand. A service spur had been erased and another crossing drawn in. Her note thanked the keeper for finding it.

His answer occupied the white space beside the new line.

*She corrected my map. The old service spur is gone, exactly as she said. She told the others I had found the route.*

Under that, the paper bore the remains of a question rubbed out so thoroughly that only the pressure survived.

I tilted it toward the window.

*Who has been here before?*

The cabin had a narrow view down all three roads. From it, one person could change the direction of a whole train without seeing any passenger’s face. Levers stood in a row beneath the window. Their handles had been polished by use, and each bore a number set into a small brass oval.

On one handle a little twist of paper had been wrapped as a grip. I unrolled it, expecting another instruction.

It was a drawing of a dog. The legs were too long, and there were five of them. Someone had gone back to darken the eye until it pierced the paper.

I sat down with the drawing in my palm.

A printed line crossed the dog’s belly. The child had drawn over it twice to restore the outline. I cupped the paper against the draught.

Until then the children had been rations and names, reasons for my sister’s choices. The dog explained nothing. Had another child laughed at the extra leg? Had its maker meant a tail?

The paper was soft from having been held.

I put it back around the lever as carefully as I could.

The letter under a carriage seat was addressed in large, uneven capitals.

*THANK YOU LAMP KEEPER.*

My sister had supplied the smaller writing beneath it, a route instruction and a reminder to leave the paper for the next watch. The people were to travel between the heavy brake and the fuel.

The keeper had written on the envelope:

*The child asked whether the lamp keeper was the one who had shut the door. She said there could be more than one.*

I read that sentence several times.

In my notebook I had written *he* beside every occurrence of *keeper*. A man with a careful hand, a ring of keys, a voice that made a form out of someone else’s fear. I had given him a face in the same careless way I had given the old traveller our dead neighbour’s face.

I turned back through the pages. The writing looked consistent to me, but I no longer knew what that settled. A title could move between people. A person could move between kinds of work. The hand that closed a door might later hold it open and still belong to the same man.

The wagons blocked the western service road. There were three: a tank wagon, a short passenger body and a heavy brake truck with a handwheel furred in rust. Beyond them the track sloped toward the marsh.

I climbed into the signal cabin and tried a lever. The points shifted below with a clank that startled birds out of the ballast. At the brake truck I released the wheel a little. It moved an inch, then another. I ran beside it, wound the wheel tight and stood panting with the train’s weight held in my hands.

It took much of the afternoon to put the wagons in order. The shortest siding would take only one; the loop would take two. Each solution I saw from the cabin became less simple when I had to walk down and push iron. Once the tank wagon rolled farther than I expected and struck a buffer hard enough to shake dust from the embankment.

The buffer held and pushed the wagon back. Seeds shook from the grass and drifted across its tank. My hands remained extended, too late to stop it. I knelt to check the coupling pin, then checked it again.

The passenger body went between the brake and the fuel.

Inside it, the empty seats faced one another.

I left the brake tightened at the downhill end and opened the service passage. The signal arm lifted against the sky. It looked purposeful, almost eager. The rails beneath it remained silent.

Before leaving, I went back for the child’s drawing.

I left a clean strip from my shirt around the lever in its place.

At the bottom of the embankment, reeds began. Beyond them lay water so still it held the evening sky without a crease.

Ballast thinned into grass, then sedge. A culvert spilled dark water through a stone arch; downstream, reeds lay bent by an older, stronger current. Pools joined around low islands where willows turned their leaves to the evening wind. Farther out, dead trunks stood rooted in both mud and reflected sky. Between them the channel curved toward the last light.

After the railway’s silence, I stopped to hear one reed brush another.

## V. Water Held Between Banks

The boat was on the other side.

I could see it through the reeds, a low dark shape tucked beneath the far bank. At first I thought someone was sitting in it. A bent cover over the stern made the shape of shoulders.

Black reeds crossed the shoulders; grayer stems moved behind them. A pale strip might have been a hand on the gunwale. I raised my own hand, called, and waited. Nothing lifted its head.

A rope ran from the near mooring through the water. I took it in both hands and pulled. It came reluctantly, shedding black droplets. Across the channel the boat moved an inch out of its shelter.

The bow sent a widening line through the reflected reeds. Planks emerged from the silhouette, then fastenings, then a repair visible when the hull tilted. The pale hand became an oar. The shoulders collapsed into canvas.

By the time the boat reached me, the light was nearly gone.

It was wider than it had looked from the bank, built to carry several people and their possessions. The benches were marked with pale arcs where wet bodies had sat. A metal cup lay under the middle seat. When I picked it up, a ring of water remained beneath it, bright for a moment against the dark wood.

I stood with one foot on the bank and one on the boat, unwilling to put my whole weight into either place.

The reeds touched one another again.

I stepped into the centre.

The boat accepted me with a slow downward movement. Water rose along its sides and stopped. Nothing dramatic happened. I had been holding my breath.

The crossing was short enough that I should not have feared it. Both banks were visible. The rope remained in my hand. Yet once the bank released us, there was no sound of passage except a soft drinking at the bow. The dark water showed no current, no depth, no reason to believe it was different a yard farther on. The oars entered it with a small resistance and disappeared.

Each blade left a smooth oval before the sky entered it again. My reflection leaned toward me when I shifted. I brought my weight back to the centre.

The far bank was a low overhang of roots and black soil. Above it, dead grasses caught the last light. Beneath it, their hanging roots made a dark fringe over a hollow I could not see into. I watched that fringe approach until I could distinguish one root with a drop at its end.

I rowed badly. One blade bit deeper than the other, turning the boat until the near bank slid away to my left. I corrected too hard. Reeds scraped the hull. When I looked over my shoulder, the far bank seemed to have moved farther back.

I remembered my sister teaching me to carry a bowl filled to its brim. “Look where you’re going,” she said. I stared at the water instead, watching each step lift it against the edge. She took the bowl from me and carried it across the kitchen without spilling a drop, her eyes on the table.

In the boat I looked at the far mooring.

The little building behind it had no roof. Two walls enclosed a bench. The other two had fallen outward into grass, leaving a doorway that admitted the whole marsh. I kept my eyes on the doorway and made myself row toward it.

When the bow touched earth, I stayed where I was.

The bank had arrived so quietly that I did not trust it.

I slept in the roofless building beneath the boat’s canvas. Before lying down I ate the remaining heel of bread and changed my stockings. My feet had become pale and swollen. There were raw places at the backs of both heels. I dried them with the inside of my sleeve and laid the wet stockings over the wall.

The boat knocked softly against its mooring through the night.

In the morning, a token hung from a nail under the bench.

It was heavy for its size: a boat pressed inside three grooves. The string was new, though the metal had been worn thin at one edge by years of being passed from hand to hand.

My sister had tied a note to it.

*I gave him what we had. He said it was enough for all of us.*

In the ferry book, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a stone, the keeper had written:

*No fare was taken. She presented a transfer permit. The boatman recognised the seal and opened the holding gate.*

I rubbed the three grooves with my thumb. They were the same grooves cut into the gate beyond the landing.

Pools and strips of sedge spread beneath a low sky, its brightness rising from the water as well as falling. Every bank promised an edge and revealed another beyond it. A lone fence post stood doubled in a pool until a ripple bent its reflection. Farther stakes faded when I looked straight at them.

The land near the path was more intimate and less forgiving. A broken reed could cut the skin. Mud clung between my toes. Small bubbles rose where my heel had been and stayed on the surface after I moved.

There was a narrow track to the lock house. I followed it with my boots in my hand, letting the cold mud take some of the heat from my feet.

The track followed a raised bank, firm over roots and soft where water had cut through. To the left, old reeds thatched a ditch; to the right, sedge gave way to mud, clear shallows and the dark channel. A passing cloud changed the shallows from water I could see through to a mirror.

Willows arched over the pools. Rafts of twigs caught at their feet; between the trunks, brief views opened and closed as I walked. A leaf turned against a submerged branch, caught again, then released into the current.

Above the flat country, a pale escarpment looked like cloud until I found a dark seam in it. The quarry. Between us, every willow and weathered post became a landmark.

Halfway along, I found the place where the others had waited.

An awning had been fastened between two posts. Under it stood a long bench and a chair with one leg sunk into the ground. Beside the chair, a groove had been worn into the planks of a small platform. It was narrow, round at the end, and repeated so many times that the wood had gone smooth.

A walking stick had rested there.

I sat on the bench and tried putting the imagined stick down in the groove.

Sackcloth cushioned the chair. I rested my elbow on its arm: the right height for a tired person to hold their head. Three sets of shoe scuffs marked the bench beside it, the smallest stopping halfway down. From the chair, the old man could have watched the channel without turning. Perhaps that was why she had put him there.

My sister’s instruction was tucked into a seam in the awning.

*Wait until everyone can stand.*

I folded it and unfolded it.

The keeper recorded two days spent here under an instruction he could not countermand. He had underlined *two*.

*She said the delay was hers. I saw the rope tied on their side of the water.*

The rope still lay there, its thick knot resting against the post.

The knot had worn an oval in the post. Its cut end was whipped with dozens of patient turns of thread. I loosened it. No lock, no wire, no hidden fastening. I tied it again.

Above the water, five marks were cut into the bank’s board. The bell rope hung beside them. Someone had tied an extra loop low enough for a child to reach.

I pulled it once.

The bell sounded from the far end of the channel. Its note spread over the marsh and diminished without an echo.

The bell’s pale rim appeared on one side, then the other. Its arc narrowed until it hung wholly dark. I kept hold of the rope.

I waited.

A small creature moved beneath the sedge, making three stems quiver. Far out, a bird lifted and flew just above its reflection. Nothing else answered.

I had intended to leave at once. Instead I remained on the bench until the damp came through my trousers.

At home, during our mother’s illness, there had been a day when my sister would not let me go for a walk. There was no particular need for me in the house. Our mother was sleeping. The water had been brought, the washing done. I had reached the gate when my sister called me back.

“I may need you.”

“Then call.”

“You won’t hear.”

We looked at each other across the garden. Her hands were wet from wringing out a cloth. I remember the water falling from them while she waited for me to obey.

I went back.

For years I had remembered that afternoon as one of her small cruelties. Sitting beside the old man’s groove, I remembered something else. When I came into the kitchen, she sat down on the floor with her back against the cupboard and covered her face. She made no sound. I had stood there feeling cheated because I could no longer be angry in the way I wanted.

The ferry book gave those two days a line. I knew how much could fit inside it: a cup lifted, a foot moved, another attempt to stand.

I put on my boots and went to the lock.

Two chambers lay between the lower marsh and a higher channel leading toward pale hills. Their walls were striped with old water levels.

The upper stripe was powdery; beneath it lay black stone, then green threads bending underwater. A ladder passed through all three. Beside a ferry cradle stood a pump, its old blue paint surviving in corners untouched by mud.

The operating sheet allowed four measures of water between the chambers, with no fresh supply. I closed the gates and worked the pump. Pressure caught beneath the handle; a float lifted. One watermark fell as the other rose. When they met, I opened the middle gate. The water scarcely moved. Equal levels had taken the violence out of the crossing. The ferry cradle drifted through under a light pull on its rope.

I rode it into the upper chamber and closed the gate behind me. A second handle worked the same pump from this side. I stood on the cradle and pulled until water rose around its hull, lifting me toward the high landing while the other chamber emptied. The upper gate opened when its watermark disappeared beneath the surface.

The wall sank past my shoulders. Grass tips appeared above it, then the raised path. The watermark passed my knees and vanished. Without taking a step, I had entered another view.

When I stepped onto the landing, I turned and looked at the boat before tying it. It was still moving almost imperceptibly upward. I waited until it settled, then left enough slack in the line for it to fall again.

From there, the marsh had changed. I could see over the reeds to the building where I slept, the little awning, the strip of bank on which seven people had waited. All of it looked quiet and easily left. I could almost believe waiting there had been a choice simply because I was no longer waiting.

At the upper landing, two sheets had dried together under a board. I separated them with water from my cup.

The same seven names appeared on each. One had been headed *released at dawn*. The other said *received at dawn*. My sister’s letters stood beside the keeper’s, both preserving the count. The larger headings had washed away.

I held the sheets up to the pale sky. Through their thin places, reeds and cloud appeared inside the names.

For a moment I could not tell which bank I was looking toward.

I pressed the papers flat between pages of the notebook and went up the path toward the quarry. The mud dried on my feet as I climbed. Behind me the little bell moved in a breath of wind, but not far enough to sound.

## VI. The Weight Left Over

The quarry had taken the side out of a hill.

I saw the exposed stone before I found the road into it: white terraces cut across the slope, each casting a thin black line beneath itself. Loose stone lay at the bottom in fans and heaps. Far above, a few trees remained along the original ridge, their roots exposed where the edge had crumbled. Against that enormous brightness, the trees looked small and precarious, like weeds in the crack of a wall.

Tool marks scored the faces. Some ran straight; others bowed where the stone had broken early. Sun bleached the loose flakes white, then a cloud restored their grain.

A crane stood far above me. Its cable descended across three terraces before ending in a hook I could scarcely distinguish. Nearer, a discarded chisel lay half buried beside the path. I stepped over it and looked again at the missing hill.

The ramp climbed diagonally above a wall of packed rubble. Wind sorted chips from powder; dusty plants pressed into the deepest joints. Across the excavation, a line of standing water revealed a bench’s inward tilt. A fissure carried seepage from the rim to the spoil, turning white stone gray, then black.

Above the cut faces, trees leaned away from the void. Their roots hung over the edge. Behind them rose an untouched hill, soft with vegetation. I climbed between that distant green and details small enough to hold: drill holes, a wedge mark, a snail shell in the dust.

Underfoot, the powder took the damp click from my soles and replaced it with a dry scuff. Soon it covered the hems of my trousers. I wiped my face and left a pale streak on my sleeve.

The first stone I moved was no larger than a barrel, but the ground made its weight treacherous. It had lodged between the path and a hollow with a steep far lip. Someone had already cut a groove for it. I pushed it along the groove, resting whenever it came against a ridge, until it rolled free and dropped into the depression.

The impact shook dust from a ledge above me.

I waited with my hands still out, as if I could call it back.

When the dust cleared, the stone made a usable step.

On the other side, a child’s pouch lay beneath a slab.

It contained six stones chosen for their likeness in size. Their surfaces had been rubbed clean, and one bore a charcoal face. Held one way, the face smiled. Turned over, it seemed to frown.

The drawn mouth followed a chip in the stone. One edge was smooth from being turned in a pocket. I turned it now, wondering which expression had come first.

My sister’s note was folded into the bottom.

*We played at moving a family across the quarry while the real lift was repaired. Keep these little stones out of the gears.*

I set them on the slab. They made a small, uneven line. Without intending to, I placed the smiling stone at the front.

The keeper’s repair report lay under the pouch.

*They knew which stones to move when she rang the bell. She called it a game. I could not get them to try another order.*

I left the stones in their line while I read. A fly settled on the largest. It rubbed its forelegs together and departed.

My sister used to make games out of anything that needed doing. During a leak in our roof, we carried bowls from room to room according to a complicated ranking of ships. A chipped basin was the admiral. The little blue cup had to be rescued every time it filled. I remembered it as a happy afternoon until, years later, she told me she had spent it terrified that the plaster above the bed would come down.

“Why didn’t you tell me?” I asked.

“What would you have done?”

I had no answer that satisfied either of us.

I gathered the six stones and put them back in the pouch. The one with the face went in last. Its expression changed as it turned between my fingers.

The path rose through ledges and ladder recesses. Iron rungs had been driven into the rock at intervals that suited someone taller than I was. At one place, three small bootprints had been pressed into a patch of old lime between the adult tracks. The smallest feet had stayed close to the wall. Along the outside edge, a strip of cloth had been tied at the height of a child’s shoulder, making a boundary where there was no rail.

I held the cloth as I passed, though it could not have borne my weight.

Above the second ladder the quarry opened beneath me. The air had cleared, and I could see the marsh beyond the cuttings, a silver strip between darker land. Farther still, the oil field’s tanks looked like dull coins laid out on a table. I had crossed that country slowly enough to feel each obstruction, yet from here it fit within the space between two ridges.

I searched it for the house.

There were too many hills in the way.

For the first time I understood that I had left home in a direction I might not be able to retrace by memory. The papers in my bag had become more than a record of her. They were the shape of the route behind me.

A gust rose along the quarry face. Dust spilled past my boots and gathered into pale sheets. The crane vanished from the base upward until only its arm remained. I covered my mouth. Chalk settled along my coat seams, drawing a paler outline around me.

The hoist stood at the edge of the upper works.

Its cage hung over a shaft, held by cables that entered a pulley frame above. Two arms extended from a central pivot, one longer than the other. Beneath them, a brake drum pressed against the cable wheel. A handle beside it had split lengthwise. Someone had bound the split in wool.

My sister’s scarf.

I knew it before I saw the backward stitch. The wool was worn thin at the place she rubbed with her thumb when she was thinking. As a child I had sometimes taken that habit as a sign that she was listening closely to me. Later I learned she did it when she was listening to something else in her own head.

I put my hand around the handle.

The cloth softened the grip. The broken wood beneath could not cut me.

For once I did not read the note immediately. I held the handle and let that one fact remain alone.

A broken stitch dangled near the turning joint. I tucked it under the next fold and closed my hand again. The grip was smoother.

Across the shaft, chalk drifted through the cage. Nothing else in the quarry seemed made to fit a hand.

When I unfolded the paper, I found the calculation she had left for the load.

*I could not let them fall.*

The keeper had checked her figures and found them right.

*She knew the setting before we weighed the cage. She called the margin unsafe, then changed it herself. I did not see a table in her hands.*

Under the notice was a clean rectangle on the machine’s casing. Something had once been fixed there. Four screw holes remained at the corners, two with broken screws still inside. I ran a finger along the edge of the clean patch.

Perhaps the missing plate had held the figures. She could have read it before someone took it away; there need be nothing more mysterious in her knowing. I looked around the base of the machine, under the brake, in the powdery dust beneath the ladder. There was no plate.

The shaft smelled of wet stone. Somewhere below, water dripped slowly enough that each drop had time to become a separate thing before the next arrived.

I was afraid of the cage.

I could make myself cross a ledge or climb a tree because each handhold offered its own reassurance. Here I would have to commit my whole body to work done elsewhere: a cable above my head, a wheel I could no longer reach, a brake whose pressure depended on weights arranged before I stepped inside.

My sister had put six people into it.

Or seven.

The torn note lay in the cage, trapped beneath a loose floorboard.

*Three children, two adults, one old man. Six are still with me. Leave it ready for whoever follows.*

The keeper had corrected her count.

*Seven, including you.*

At the torn edge, her next line began:

*When I am no longer with them—*

I knelt on the cage floor and looked through the gaps between the boards. There was nothing below but darkness broken by the pale face of one projecting stone.

I had counted every mile as a mile nearer to her. Looking down between the boards, I understood that the others might have continued without her. They could have passed her instructions from hand to hand while she remained somewhere behind me. All that climbing, and I might have been walking away.

I stood too quickly and struck my shoulder against the cage frame.

The pain helped. It gave me something I could put a hand over.

The weights were stacked on the brake tray. Six equal iron blocks, each with a recessed handle. I carried them one at a time to the arms. The longer arm fell under less weight than I expected. The shorter needed more. When I had two blocks on one side and three on the other, the beam steadied.

One block remained on the brake.

I tested the handle. The wheel turned reluctantly, the wool pressing into my palm. I lowered the empty cage a little, stopped it, raised it again. Then I did it once more. I watched the cable all the way up to the pulley, searching for loose wires.

At last I climbed inside. A brake cord ran down through a guide beside the frame.

One floorboard was new, rough with saw marks. I put both feet on it. The cage tilted and forced me to move one foot away; darkness showed between my boots. I tried the brake cord, let it return, then took it in both hands and eased the brake clear.

The cage moved with a shudder that passed through my knees. The quarry wall slid past, close enough to brush. Dark seams opened in the white stone, each containing damp earth and fine roots. Halfway down I saw a tiny fern growing where a trickle crossed the rock. Its leaves were so green that I forgot to be afraid for a moment.

The newest frond was still curled. I watched it rise past my face and vanish above the frame. After that I searched every seam for another.

The cage reached the lower gallery and settled against its stop.

I stepped out. Then I went back, touched the floor with my boot, and looked upward at the dark square through which I had descended.

Seven, including you.

I could hear that sentence in a kind voice. I could hear it in a clerk’s voice. Both followed me down the gallery.

The gallery narrowed. Behind me the quarry became a bright, uneven doorway, reflected in a thread of water along the floor. Chisel marks caught the last of its light.

At the far end, dust gave way to damp stone and ferns. Broken glasshouses stepped down terraces in the quarry’s lee, sheltered between its shoulder and a wooded ridge. Warmth rose near the first doorway. I stood there with my sore feet apart, grateful beyond thought.

## VII. Things Still Growing
The smell reached me first: damp soil, bruised leaves, the faint sharpness of something green when its stem is broken.

After the quarry, it seemed almost extravagant.

Glasshouse ribs curved over the beds. Empty clips held splinters of glazing; surviving panes reflected sky, an inverted row of plants, my sleeve. The roof seemed fuller when I moved beneath it.

I parted the thorns at the doorway with my forearm. They caught my coat, released it, and closed behind me.

The beds were alive.

Three rows stepped with the hillside, from half-glazed rooms to bare frames over open beds. Bramble crowded the outer walls. Inside, an ivy trail crossed the planting rows and climbed a bench leg, where someone had cut it. The dead upper leaves still clung there.

Limewashed brick stood green at its foot, gray beneath broken gutters. Pipes ran at ankle height, their joints wrapped in damp felt. Low sunlight threaded the leaves while the soil beneath the benches was already black.

Someone had stretched strings over the seedlings and drawn shallow channels to their roots. A watering can stood on bricks; a root had found the wet circle beneath it. The paths were swept. A broken pot sheltered a new shoot from the draught.

I knelt and touched a leaf.

Quarry dust left a pale crescent on the leaf. I wetted my finger and lifted it away. The leaf bent under the touch, then rose.

I smiled. After so many remains, that small resistance felt intimate. I laid my palm on the soil.

Warm.

Pipes ran beneath the beds. At the end of the house a thick return entered the ground, its surface sweating where it passed through cooler air. Beside it stood a water barrel and a row of cups. A tap above the barrel dripped into a basin so slowly that a little mound of mineral had formed where each drop struck.

I drank, waited, drank again.

There was food in a cupboard under the workbench: a sealed jar of dried beans, several hard biscuits, roots laid in sand. I took one biscuit and held it between both hands. The first bite hurt my dry mouth. I ate too quickly anyway, then stood still until the cramp in my stomach eased.

The cupboard door had a list on the inside. Portions, days, numbers of people. My sister’s writing ran across the bottom.

*Keep enough to begin again.*

I shut the door gently.

In the next house, seven sleeping places had been made between the beds. Straw pallets lay on wooden frames raised just clear of the warm soil. Someone had cut a curtain from old sacking and strung it between two posts. Behind it were a basin, a towel dried stiff, and a little shelf carrying a comb with three teeth missing.

Uneven stitches joined the sacking; a stone weighted one crooked corner. Behind it a person would be visible only by their feet. I pressed a clean fold of the towel to my cheek and left a print of quarry dust.

I lingered in that improvised privacy.

There were curtains at home we had never hung properly. One corner sagged because the hook had come away from the plaster. My sister used to catch it back with a pin whenever she passed. I had resented being reminded of the repair by her small, repeated act. Here she had found a wall where there was no wall, a room inside a ruined building, and I wanted to lie down behind the sacking and let her arrange the rest of the world.

I chose the pallet farthest from the door.

Then I saw the lock.

It was on the inside. A heavy iron box bolted to the frame, its catch reachable without going out into the cold. The key was gone. A wedge kept the door open now, but the floor beneath it bore a wide scrape from repeated closing.

One pallet lay just beyond the door’s worn arc. I lifted the wedge. As the door moved, the bright strip on the floor narrowed and the nearest leaves lost their shine. I caught it and pushed the wedge back.

The keeper’s inventory lay on the workbench.

*They had food, heat and beds. She kept the key and told them the cold would kill anyone who left.*

He had written the temperatures beneath the accusation. Low figures, each followed by a neat degree mark. Several were so low that I read them twice.

My hand went back to the warm pipe.

That evening I moved a waterlogged tray out of a drip and shimmed it level with scraps of wood. I tied a stem with thread from my cuff, then turned seedlings leaning too far toward the light.

The tasks quieted me. There was no date to compare, no hidden instruction in the wet weight of a pot. If I lifted a fallen stem, it stood. If I moved a tray into the light, the light reached it.

I found the count while I was turning the trays.

Each row had a narrow slate at its end. The figures had been copied onto paper and clipped to a wire. Two consecutive mornings showed the same correction. A six had been crossed out and made into a seven, with a little hook where the pen hesitated.

The hook was identical.

Not merely similar. The same slight fork, the same dot at its base, the same empty place where the line had failed to join itself.

I carried both sheets to the last clear pane. Night was gathering outside, turning the glass into a weak mirror. My face appeared behind the figures.

I held one sheet over the other. The corrections covered each other exactly.

Her note beside the count said:

*Write tomorrow’s count even if I am gone. Someone must know what is still growing.*

The keeper had added:

*Ask who held the pen.*

I thought of the city lamp, the wheel lifting its metal tongue. The light had been no less bright because the chair beside it was empty. These plants were no less alive because a count might have been copied. I wanted that to be enough.

My hands had begun to shake. I pressed the counts beneath an empty pot and went back to the seedlings.

In a drawer under the workbench I found a maintenance tag for a writing apparatus. Its illustration showed a jointed arm following the grooves of a plate. The arm was drawn without ornament, the way a hinge or a drain fitting might be drawn. Someone had circled the word *master*.

I shut the drawer.

There was still warmth. There was still food. I could remain here for a day and let my feet heal. I could leave the papers until morning.

The smallness of what I wanted frightened me. Two days beside the marsh had seemed an intolerable delay when I read about them. Now I would have given an hour of my sister’s unknown time for the right to lie still.

I ate a second biscuit and lay down.

The straw crackled beneath me. Through the broken roof I could see a patch of sky darkening between iron ribs.

The arches stood black against the ridge. Between two trees, the last pale strip of evening remained. A leaf near my pallet turned in the rising warmth, appearing and vanishing without changing its place.

Water moved in the pipe beside my bed with a low, irregular murmur. Now and then a cooling pane made a delicate sound, as if someone had touched its edge with a fingernail.

I thought about my sister asleep.

She slept with one hand folded under her cheek. In the morning a crease would remain there from the sheet or from her own knuckles. I had watched that crease fade while she made tea. It was one of the things no letter had ever told me about her because it had seemed impossible that I would need a letter to remember it.

I tried to picture the crease now.

Her face would not hold still. The younger face I remembered from the grass and the tired face in our doorway slid across each other. Then the face in the dream returned, patient on the carriage seat, waiting for me to see what I had left out.

I turned toward the pipe and slept.

In the morning, light lay across the beds in long pale bars. The air had cooled, and the glass at the lower end of the house was covered in condensation. A child's drawing hung beside an older card on the far wall.

The lantern in the drawing was larger than the woman who carried it.

Hard strokes of light passed through the six following figures; the child had drawn them again to bring them back. The woman’s feet pointed in different directions. On the older card both feet stood neatly on a printed line. The child’s woman wore her scarf short on one side.

Underneath, my sister had written:

*They promised they would keep it lit. I did not ask them to promise.*

On the older card, brown ink arranged the same woman, lantern and six figures. Its print date preceded the cuts in the forest. Even the scarf was short on one side.

The keeper’s note was brief.

*The picture came before the promise.*

I held the child's drawing closer. The second figure had been rubbed out and redrawn taller. One person’s hand had too many fingers. In the printed card, the figures stood at equal intervals. In the drawing, the smallest leaned against the woman’s skirt.

I took out the dog from the railway and set it beside them.

I looked for an eye, an extra finger, some mistake that would join the two drawings to one child. Then I caught myself and put the dog away.

At the back of the house, a glass partition blocked the passage toward the dam. Moisture covered the lower receiver of its latch. An upper shutter controlled the vent. My sister had drawn the light’s route in the margin of the child’s picture, using the lantern rays as pointers.

I followed them.

The first mirror was tarnished at its edges. I turned it and morning light climbed the wall, catching dust in its path. Two more adjustments brought the beam to the upper shutter.

A falling drop crossed the beam and flashed. At the upper receiver, a catch lifted.

Cold air entered above my head. It moved through the house slowly, touching one row of leaves and then another. The condensation began to clear. Individual drops grew visible, joined, slid down the pane.

Through it, I could see the path onward.

I redirected the mirrors toward the lower receiver. The latch clicked. The partition opened far enough to admit a person.

Before leaving, I went back to the cupboard. I took food for the road and wrote down what I had taken. The pencil was blunt. My fingers, though cleaner, remained stiff.

I put a wrong number in the second column.

For a long moment I stared at it.

Then I crossed it out and wrote the right one. There was no way to make the correction that did not look deliberate.

At the sleeping places I folded the blanket I had used. I left the seedling I had tied standing straight. I took the wedge from under the outer door, thought of the temperatures in the keeper’s book, and put it back where it had been.

The warmth followed me only a little way down the path.

## VIII. The Common Supply

I heard the dam before I saw it.

For the first mile the sound might have been wind in trees. Then it acquired weight. It came through the soles of my boots when I stepped onto the old service pavement, through a handrail when I steadied myself, through the air behind my teeth. By the time the path turned, I had begun to carry my shoulders high against it.

Drops fringed the rail though no rain fell. Beside a drain, a puddle trembled. The water had begun to move everything before I could see it.

The wall filled the valley.

The dam joined a wooded shoulder to a sheer rock face. Above its crown lay a pale strip of impounded water; below, a broad basin turned out of sight with the valley. Driftwood marked an older waterline along the bank. Beneath it the stone was clean and dark; above it, silver lichen resumed.

Across the basin, ladders joined landings small as notches. Galleries vanished behind buttresses and reappeared at other heights. I could assemble pieces of a route, never the whole passage.

Water fell in four broad sheets from openings near its crown. At a distance they appeared motionless, pale fabric hung against black stone. Nearer, their surfaces broke into restless ridges, and I could see the immense downward movement that the scale had concealed. Spray drifted from their feet and lay over the reservoir like smoke.

Spray passed in low sheets, white against the bank’s dark recesses and almost invisible over sunlit stone. The mountains beyond rose in ranks: trees in the nearest clefts, bare slopes farther off, then ridges visible only against bright cloud. A high notch held snow. It looked small, but the trees beneath it had vanished with distance.

I looked back to the wall, its galleries and doors, then down to one pitted pebble in the parapet beneath my hand. My sleeve fluttered against the rail.

At the turbine shed, the ground trembled. A lamp burned above a gauge whose glass had been replaced with a sheet of clear mica.

Scratches doubled the gauge needle until I shifted my head. Triangles marked the cold-start load; round marks lay lower. My sister’s writing curved around the casing. I had to move to finish reading it.

*I restored power for the signal. It held through the night. You cannot ask people to wait in darkness.*

There was a drawing on the wall beside it.

One wire left a switch, travelled a little way, and divided.

The keeper had marked one branch *lamps*. The other he had labelled *recorder*.

*She knew they shared the feed.*

I stood close enough that the vibration passed from the wall into my shoulder.

In the gardens I had been able to put the maintenance tag away. Here the apparatus had become a line I could follow with my finger, a physical connection between the lights and the writing. The switch beside me was large and ordinary. Its handle had room for one hand.

Outside, the service route climbed through masonry ledges.

The ledges followed the rock, passing under overhangs and over small masonry bridges. Mineral teeth hung beneath them. In sheltered seams, ferns and tiny round leaves grew against wet stone. A shrub rooted sideways above the path; its shadow crossed my sleeve.

At one turn the reservoir came into view beneath me, an expanse of dull pewter pressed between the dam and the valley’s far shoulder.

A boat waited at the bottom of the steps.

This one was narrow, built for the dam workers, with a shallow centre and a single pair of oars. A chain fastened it to a ring in the wall. Its bow lifted and fell with the reflected movement of distant water.

The landing on the far side seemed impossibly small.

A buttress concealed half the landing. From the boat’s height only wet stone and the top rungs showed. One step sideways hid them too. I stepped back.

There was no rope across this water to hold while I rowed. Beyond the sheltered inlet, the reservoir opened toward the spillway. I could not see the current, but leaves moved through a seam on the surface and disappeared beneath a haze of spray.

I waited until I could tell which way they went.

The boat smelled of wet wood and old rope. I stepped into its middle, keeping low. A repair across the floor had been made with a darker plank, shaped carefully to the hull. On one side, a place where the gunwale had split was bound with wire covered in cloth so it would not catch the rower’s sleeve.

I rested my forearms on the padding and felt two worn hollows fit them. Water lay in a seam beneath the seat. A leaf turned there, its stem caught against a nail.

I put the bag under the seat, lifted it again, and set it between my boots. My fingers checked the closure twice.

I touched the binding and did not look for a stitch.

The oars were heavy. I took the first strokes cautiously, expecting the boat to resist as the marsh ferry had. Instead it slid away from the wall with alarming ease. The steps receded. The ring to which the chain had been fastened became a small black eye in the stone.

As I moved out, the wall seemed to rise.

Doorways I had taken for full-sized shrank against it. The four spill sheets widened above me. The boat’s sides enclosed only my knees, my bag, the narrow board under my feet. Everything beyond them was water, stone and falling water.

Along the closest spill sheet, the surface divided into thick translucent cords before breaking into spray. Those cords seemed to descend slowly until a single darker fragment—a leaf or a piece of bark—entered them and vanished. At their foot, white turbulence spread outward in ridges. Farther from the wall, the ridges grew lower, passed beneath the boat and lifted it with a delayed, indifferent strength.

As the boat rose and fell, the landing appeared and vanished. My fingers whitened on the oars. The hull was a brief crease in all that water.

I rowed.

Near the middle, a gust came down the valley and turned the surface dark. The bow shifted. I pulled hard on one oar and caught the blade badly; the handle struck my ribs. For a moment the boat lay broadside to the little waves.

The next ridge of water folded over the gunwale, struck my boot and ran between the boards. I brought one knee over the bag. An oar swung across the empty seat where another person might have caught it.

I dropped the oar and caught it by the shaft before it left the boat. The other blade dragged, turning us farther. My bag slid against my ankle. I clamped it between my feet and lowered my body until my chest almost touched my knees.

The boat rocked once, twice, then found its balance.

I stayed folded over my knees, watching the nailheads rock against the far wall. When they steadied, I raised my head. The land went on leaning.

The far landing had moved to the side of my field of view. I could not make my hands do anything useful while I looked at it.

I looked at the next patch of water instead.

One stroke. Then another. Shorter this time, close to the hull. The boat began to turn. I kept going until the landing was where it should be and then refused to lift my eyes above it.

When I reached the ring, I missed it with the chain.

The boat drew away. I caught the edge of the step with my fingertips, losing skin against the rough stone, and held us there while I tried again. When the chain finally went through, I wound it twice and sat in the bottom of the boat, water soaking into the seat of my trousers.

Above me the dam continued without noticing.

I climbed onto the steps. Through the iron treads, the boat rose and fell, drawing its chain taut. I tried to close my scraped fingers. The middle two were slow to obey.

I began to laugh, a short breathless sound that hurt my ribs. Then I was crying. I did neither for long. The cold gave me work to do.

On the steps I wrung water from my coat and inspected the notebook. Its outer pages had darkened, but the oilcloth I had taken from the ferry book had protected the centre. I spread it on a dry ledge, using the knife to hold the pages down.

There were white marks on my fingers where the oar had pressed.

I wanted her there with an unreasonable violence. Not her instructions, not the scarf, not the cleverness that anticipated where I would need a ladder. I wanted her to see the place where the wood had struck me and say it would bruise. I wanted to be angry at her while she was present to answer.

Instead there was a pipe.

It ran from the next valve chamber back toward the gardens. Its broad surface was colder than the one beside my pallet had been. The route label was partly obscured by lime. I scraped it clean with a fingernail.

My sister had tied a note to the valve wheel.

*Do not trade their heat for my call. If someone demands more for the ridge, show them this.*

I read it while holding my coat shut against the wind.

The keeper’s account lay in the maintenance box. He wrote that she controlled the common supply, that refusal meant losing the warmth on which the people below depended. Folded behind his accusation was a request for extra power to the ridge.

It bore his signature.

I laid the sheets side by side on the valve cover. Rain began stippling the paper, and I pulled them under shelter.

For much of the journey I had heard his writing as though it came from outside events. A man observing, recording, refusing her descriptions. His signature on the request placed his hand on a valve of his own.

The rain thickened. Far across the reservoir, a white seam appeared in the clouds. For an instant the ridges beyond the dam became sharply visible, each folded slope exposed.

Light exposed the farthest gullies, whitened the falls, flashed along a row of bolts. The papers shone in my hands; I lowered them as though caught reading. Then the brightness left and thunder joined the water.

I climbed.

The ladders were bolted to the wet wall. Their rungs held small pools at the welds. Between them the galleries narrowed, sometimes running beneath overhangs that kept off the rain, sometimes crossing open faces where gusts pushed spray up from below. I kept the notebook inside my coat and moved with one arm pressed against it.

At the upper gallery, a service rope crossed a broken span. Its knot was doubled around an iron eye set into the wall. The rope’s outer fibres had worn pale where hands passed. I held it against my chest and moved across without looking at the reservoir.

Another flash came while I was suspended.

The white wall seemed to jump nearer, then withdraw.

My shadow leapt across the wall: a bent knee, an extended leg, hands locked above my head. After the flash I could no longer locate the ledge. I hung twisting until ordinary gray returned.

On the ledge I found a recorder manual inside a metal cabinet.

The pages were protected by a rubber seal. Their dryness made them seem new, though the corners had been handled. The diagram from the gardens appeared again, larger: a writing arm, a master plate, a selector advanced by a live electrical feed.

The keeper had written across the explanation.

*A signature proves a pattern, not a person.*

I shut my eyes.

I could see the blot in the city log. The doubled correction among the seedlings. The first note folded into the scarf. My sister’s words had been the nearest thing to her body that I could reach. Now the possibility of metal joints and a moving stylus entered each line.

Her message lay on the next page.

*Leave my words for the next watch. I cannot be in every place that needs an answer.*

I read it aloud. The sound of my own voice inside the cabinet was startlingly close.

Farther on, the manual showed a glass enclosure with two interlocked faces. Cutting the feed released the master compartment and shut the live selector. Completing it exposed the pen and sealed the original plate. One release drove the other closed.

I traced the linkage with the tip of my damaged knife.

Two shutters shared one arm. My knife followed a dotted arc to a closed face, then returned to the other. A clean thumbprint marked the dusty margin. Someone else had studied this choice.

Rain drained from the gallery roof. The storm had begun to pass, leaving the air colder and the valley strangely distinct. I could see all the way back to the opposite landing, where the steps were no larger than marks cut into a stone.

At the distributor, the cold return had lost its prime. The garden line needed filling before its smaller running share would hold. I closed the ridge bypass and gave the turbine and return the cold-start load. The wheel resisted, then loosened. Deep inside the wall, water struck an empty space and filled it.

I waited with my palm on the pipe.

It warmed by degrees.

Condensation cleared beneath my palm and dwindled along the pipe toward the gardens. I pictured warm air stirring the leaves beside my pallet. When the pressure steadied, I reduced the return and opened the ridge supply. The gauge settled over the running marks. A relay began clicking behind the wall.

For a few seconds I could hear it beneath the water.

Then I could not.

I left the distributor with the gardens warm and the ridge powered. I knew what the second feed might wake. I had turned it anyway.

At the end of the gallery, the path rose toward the pass. The cloud had torn open above the highest ridge, leaving a narrow strip of pale evening sky. Against it, the first signal mast stood as thin as a pin.

## IX. The Answering Ridge

I spent the night in a maintenance shelter below the pass, with the sound of the dam reduced to a pressure in the dark.

There was a bunk fixed to the wall. I lay on it in my damp clothes because I could not make myself take them off. In the coldest part of the night I woke and put the dry stockings on my hands.

The stockings hung beyond my fingers. I tucked them beneath my chin and turned whenever my bruised ribs touched the bunk. By morning the cloth held dark knuckle marks.

At dawn, ice had formed in the washbasin.

I broke it with a finger and drank from underneath.

Outside, the higher slopes had taken snow.

Grass pierced the new snow. A filled hollow looked smoother than the ground around it; I skirted it and found the path by overturned stones, their undersides dark against weathered silver rock.

Above a narrow ravine, which I crossed by a hanging line, the trees shortened into clumps. Wind stripped their exposed faces and packed twigs into the lee. Higher still, shrubs crouched below my boots. More sky showed at every turn.

Snow rested on sloping rock shelves. I stepped between dark corners where earlier feet had broken the crust. Each breath briefly hid the next foothold.

The air tasted clean after the wet stone and oil. Each breath hurt a little. I walked with my head down until my legs warmed, then stopped to look back.

The dam lay below cloud.

I could hear it but no longer see it. Somewhere under that soft white surface were the boat, the distributor, the pipes I had filled. The gardens would be farther down, buried from sight by several folds of land. My small repairs continued without needing me to watch them.

The idea should have pleased me. Instead I thought of the unattended lamp in the city.

The first shelter post had a strip of scarf tied below an old guy wire.

This was the larger piece. The cut edge was uneven, as though she had used a poor blade or been in a hurry. Wind had frayed it, but where it rested against the post the weave was still tight. I put two fingers beneath the fold and found the backward stitches.

For a moment I let myself feel only the cloth.

Then I untied it.

The post held a tracing beneath a metal cap. On the tracing, a tower rose from a low cluster of roofs. The drawing was precise: floors, cable runs, a cabinet marked with two faces.

Beside the master compartment, a small backward mark interrupted the ruled lines. I spread the scarf over the tracing and held the two turns side by side: one in thread, one in ink.

The date was earlier than the first holding lock.

The keeper had written beneath it:

*I thought I was following an order from elsewhere.*

The paper shook in the wind. I could see through it to the darker shape of my hand.

At home my sister marked cloth with a soft pencil before she cut it. I used to ask why she bothered when she could sew a straight line without looking. “Because straight isn't the same as right,” she said. Once she cut a sleeve wrong despite the mark and sat laughing on the floor, holding it up to her shoulder. I had loved her more in that moment than when she made anything properly.

Looking at the tracing, I tried to remember whether she had ever drawn a machine.

There were years of her life I knew only from the times she came home tired. I knew how she took off her shoes. I knew which cup she chose. I had let those things stand for knowledge of what happened before she entered the room.

The scarf tugged at my wrist.

I wrapped it around my neck, inside my own collar.

It was not enough to make me warm, but I became reluctant to take it off.

The path steepened. Four shelter posts climbed the slope at uneven heights, each cut with a notch beneath its cap. Beyond the second, the world opened abruptly. The near ridge dropped away, and a whole country of darker slopes appeared beneath the white sky. Thin ribbons of water lay between them. Far out, where distance had almost removed the distinction between land and air, a cluster of uprights might have been the city.

I stopped on the exposed ledge.

All the way here, the path had given me one place at a time. Now I could see how the places occupied the same land. Water fell from these heights to the dam, ran down to the gardens, was held and lifted through locks, passed the feet of the quarry and spread through the marsh. Tracks and service roads followed the openings the land allowed. A person could call that a way through. A person with locks could make it the only way through.

A gust struck the ledge. I went down on one knee and waited with my hand against the rock.

Sleet began, first as small hard grains that struck my coat and bounced. Within minutes it came slantwise, whitening the windward sides of the stones. The distant country closed. I could see the next post and part of the one beyond it. I walked between those two things, giving up the rest.

At the shelter, I had to push snow away from the lower edge of the door.

A rock shoulder protected the shelter. Windward, clear ice glazed the stone; in its lee, soft snow preserved the shapes of buried grass. Slabs weighted the roof, with black gaps where some had shifted.

Beyond it, sleet revealed the gully in fragments: a dark face, a white ledge, exposed roots. The mountain had shrunk to places I could reach before losing sight of the last.

Inside, a flat stone held papers against a narrow shelf. My sister’s note was short.

*There was no other way.*

The keeper’s writing beneath it was the first of his I had seen lose its order. The lines sloped. Words pressed together and then spread apart.

*I believed she was repeating what she had been told. Then she corrected the order before I opened the envelope. I no longer know who gave it.*

I sat on the floor because there was nowhere else to sit.

Wind passed the shelter with a high, continuous scraping. Through a gap under the door, sleet entered one grain at a time and melted on the dark boards. I watched each little point of white diminish.

I spread the papers on the floor: her rations beside his conditions, her map beside his correction. The tracing lay nearest the door’s light. Soon they occupied every place I might have stretched my legs.

At last the hands became textures I could no longer read. I wanted her to reach in and take a page back, angry at what I had made of it. I wanted him to point out what I had missed. The doorway stayed empty.

I took out the dog drawing.

The paper had suffered in the boat. One corner had gone soft and folded over. I smoothed it against the notebook and held it there while it dried. The extra leg remained.

Then I read the keeper’s sentence again.

I could imagine his hand shaking as he wrote it. That was new. Until then I had pictured him seated comfortably whenever he made a record, with a level table and dry paper. Here the marks looked like the ones I made on my knees. I found myself wondering whether he had slept in this shelter, whether he had listened to the same thin scrape along the door and expected someone to come.

There was an old burn on the floor beside me, shaped like the base of a lamp.

I put my hand over it.

The sleet stopped as gradually as it had begun. Water replaced the scraping sound. Light entered through the roof seams, pale and uncertain. I ate one of the biscuits from the gardens and left the crumbs for whatever had made a small hole at the bottom of the wall.

When I stepped out, the higher ridge was visible.

A light flashed there.

One. Two. Three.

My mouth opened before any thought reached it.

“Wait.”

It came out quietly, as if I were still at the kitchen window. I said it louder, though the distance made it useless.

The ridge answered with three flashes.

I began to climb too quickly. My feet slipped in the slush; the bruise at my ribs pulled with each breath. At the next rise I had to lean against a post, bent over, with my hands on my thighs.

A strip of tin nailed to the post turned in the wind.

On one side, my sister had scratched:

*Do not answer your own reflection.*

The other side carried a circuit drawing. One line went out toward the ridge. Another returned. The keeper had marked the return with an arrow pointing inward.

*Three flashes alone cannot establish who is at the far end.*

The light flashed again.

The tin’s wet edge caught the distant flash. I lowered it, then raised it. At the next flash both shone together. I had been holding out a reflection as though it could answer.

I counted the interval. The same pause. The same three brief openings. I looked at the cable running below the path and saw the fresh bindings where it had been joined after a break.

I had restored the supply that morning, below the pass.

For a while I stood without moving, letting the cold find the warm places beneath my coat.

Then I followed the wire to its isolator.

The housing was stiff with ice. I warmed the catch in my hands until it moved. Inside, the inward line passed through a little hinged contact. I lifted it clear with the dry handle of the knife.

The next set of flashes did not come.

The ridge stayed gray. An afterimage brightened when I blinked, then faded. Beside me, the open contact showed a silver underside, a fraction of an inch from its mate.

I waited for another interval.

Nothing.

A contact opened; the circuit ceased to answer itself. The mast stood against the clearing sky. Something in me went on running toward it after my body understood.

I closed the housing without reconnecting the line.

The outgoing path remained.

At the signal stand, four chimes hung beneath a cover, their rods corresponding to the shelter posts’ different heights. My sister had left the reading direction in the scarf’s note. I went through the sequence slowly. With the return isolated, no inward strike confused the next. The distant crossing latch released with a dull mechanical report carried down the wire’s supports.

No light told me anyone had heard.

I went across.

My prints broke the sleet on the crossing boards. From the far support I looked back at the lamp. Its dark glass reflected sky; beneath it stood only a bracket, a bolt, a cable. There was no room for someone to wait.

The ridge path narrowed between two snow-filled hollows. In the failing afternoon, their surfaces looked smooth enough to lie on. I kept to the stones. At the highest point, I turned once more toward the country behind me.

Cloud drifted across it in long bands. A patch of water shone and disappeared. Far down, something caught the light with a brightness too small to name.

I could no longer use brightness as evidence of a person.

I looked at it anyway.

On the far side of the pass, roofs appeared below a single mast.

The descending path crossed stone ribs and grass-filled troughs threaded with meltwater. Snow lingered in shade. Lower down, shrubs returned, then small trees.

The settlement occupied a shelf above a steep drop. Roofs followed its contour in uneven rows, divided by retaining walls and dark slots of alley. Above the houses the mast stood in a web of guy wires. Beyond them the valley filled with dusk, ridge behind paler ridge.

Lights burned among the houses. Some were steady; others seemed to waver as the wind moved the last mist between us.

I descended with my sister’s scarf under my collar and my own lamp unlit in my bag.

## X. The Original

The settlement had been built into the hillside, one roof almost level with the next house’s door. Retaining walls held strips of earth in which low plants grew. Steps turned between the buildings, disappearing under arches and emerging beside cisterns. From above, it had looked like a handful of roofs. Inside it, the passages concealed one another so thoroughly that I could never tell how near I was to the next street.

Whitewash clung in the joints of rough stone. At each corner a wall hid the hillside; then a gap revealed roofs, a crooked chimney, half a lit window. Farther downhill the lights outlasted the shapes of the houses.

I walked near the middle of each passage. At the corners I slowed and leaned forward before showing the rest of myself. The habit had come upon me somewhere along the route. Only here, among doorways a person might actually step through, did I notice how cautiously I had learned to arrive.

There were lamps behind windows.

The houses crowded together until an outcrop forced them apart. Roof water ran beside the steps and under cracked stone covers. Above, whitewash shone beneath the eaves; at shoulder height, passing bodies had worn it away.

Small plots occupied the terraces. A dead vine bent a trellis; another bed had been freshly turned. One doorway was swept, the next drifted with leaves. Above them the tower rose in darker, evenly cut stone, its windows sunk deep. I looked up toward the mast, then had to find the steps again.

At the first house I stopped and listened. The lower pane was clouded from within. A pale rectangle shone on the sill, illuminating a dead moth and a crack in the paint.

A clear channel through the condensation showed a vertical edge inside. I leaned toward it; the edge moved too. A reflected doorpost. I knocked with my bare knuckles.

The sound was louder than I intended.

I waited until I began to feel foolish, then waited longer because foolishness was a poor reason to leave someone unheard.

Nothing moved behind the glass.

Farther up the steps, a wash line stretched between two walls. Its clothespins remained clipped at intervals along the cord. A shallow bowl stood upside down on a doorstep, held there by a stone. Beside another door, the boot scraper had been cleared of mud recently enough that the little heap retained its shape.

I could not tell what *recently* meant anymore.

The mast stood on a terrace above the houses. Three lamps were fixed along the rail beneath it. The first, on the left, carried a paper bound against the weather.

*I have left you a way to answer.*

Her handwriting looked calm.

Below it, the keeper had written:

*Check the arrows before you call the windows witnesses.*

The returns were marked inward. Cables left the mast and followed the walls into the houses. One entered the window where I had knocked.

I stood looking down the steps.

There might still be people inside. I was sick of what a light could neither prove nor rule out.

The tower door opened onto a stair whose centre had been worn hollow by feet. Along one wall, a cable ran in metal clips. Along the other, damp had darkened the plaster into shapes that looked like maps until I tried to follow them.

On the first landing I found seven hooks and one more added at a different height. Below them was a bench.

I stopped so suddenly that the bag swung against my hip.

Seven hooks, an eighth fixed later. It might have meant nothing beyond an extra coat. I looked for marks beneath them, for a strip of scarf, for a scratched initial. There was only old wood and a pale square where something had sheltered the wall.

I sat on the bench before going farther.

White chips surrounded the added hook. I touched one and it fell to powder on the bench.

My feet hurt less while I sat. My ribs hurt more. The scarf had dried against my neck and become rough. I took it off, smoothed it on my knee, and saw how little of it was left. Pieces of this one ordinary thing had become a trail through a country. At home she would have mended it rather than buy another. Here she had cut it until it could no longer do the simplest work of keeping her warm.

I had been wearing the remainder.

I folded it and put it inside my coat.

Higher in the tower, a gallery opened over a narrow channel. The settlement continued on the opposite slope, its foundations reflected in dark water. A service boat lay against the near landing. I crossed in it slowly, without the panic of the reservoir. The oars dipped, the bow turned, the opposite steps drew near. My hands knew something they had not known before.

That small competence made me want to cry again.

On the far side I climbed through an older portion of the tower, where the walls were thicker and the windows no wider than my shoulders. Light from the houses entered in separate strips. Moving from one to the next, I passed repeatedly through brightness and shadow.

The radio card lay under a brass clip beside the last stair.

*Three short flashes from the first lamp. Release between each. At the glass, you will know me by the correction. You have seen me make it a hundred times.*

The keeper had added only four words beneath that reassurance.

*Corrections are copied too.*

Under the card was a service record. The master plate had been sealed on the first day. The holding locks were fitted on the fourth.

He had signed for the locks.

The signature was smaller than the one on the request for power. At its end the pen had stopped hard enough to pierce the paper.

I took the record with me.

The cabinet stood in a room at the top.

It was not large. I had imagined something that justified the journey by its size: a machine filling the tower, a wall of instruments, a prison visible from the door. Instead there was a glass case on a heavy table, a sheet of paper beneath it, and a pen held in a jointed brass arm.

The pen pointed down, just clear of the page.

Two brass joints held the nib above an indentation in the paper. Ink beaded at its tip. I leaned close and the window’s reflection moved across the bead. The pen stayed still.

Beside the cabinet, two handles shared a single slotted plate. One was black, the other white. Their linkages entered opposite sides of the case. The master compartment lay behind a shutter at the back; the live pen faced the room. A narrow window showed the edge of the original plate, crossed by a dated seal.

There were no chairs.

The table was bolted to the floor. Before the controls, two hollows marked a worn mat. I stopped short of them. Fingerprints clouded the lower glass; at face height someone had wiped it clean.

I set down my bag and walked around the table.

On the far side, a rubbing of the cabinet face had been pinned to the wall. My sister’s message lay across it in the familiar, slightly sloping lines.

*Finish what I could not. There is a place for you here. You know which errors I make when I need you to notice.*

For a moment the room seemed to contract around those words.

There is a place for you here.

She had said it when we were children and I could not sleep. Her bed stood against the wall beneath the window. She would move without waking fully, raising an arm to leave a space along the warm edge. I climbed in carefully because she complained about cold feet. In the morning she would deny having invited me and move over to let me stay another minute.

I put my palm flat on the glass.

My reflection laid a hand over the page. The brass arm seemed to emerge from my wrist. I drew back, broke the illusion, then moved close enough to restore it.

The keeper’s message was lower down.

*Do not confuse her words with her will. I locked the doors. I thought I was stopping the system. She said there was no other way. I do not know whose sentence that was.*

I read it standing close enough to see my own breath faintly cloud the case.

The room’s one broad window looked over the settlement.

Roofs concealed the ways between the houses. Beyond their small, uneven windows, darkness filled the valley beneath a gray ridge. Leaning close to the glass revealed a lower lane without lamps; stepping back hid it beneath the sill.

Beneath the window, the cable entered the wall and branched toward the cabinet. It was a simple arrangement. I had seen pieces of it everywhere.

I went back down to the first lamp.

The steps felt longer in descent. I passed the hooks, the bench, the window where the cable entered. At the terrace the evening had become clear. Across the valley, the pass was a black line beneath the remaining light.

I pressed the lamp contact once, released it, pressed again, released, pressed a third time.

Three short flashes.

The old gesture travelled through my hand before I thought about it. In the long grass I had used the mirror clumsily, flaring light in every direction. Here the machine made the signal clean.

Inside the tower, something unlocked.

I waited at the rail. No person came to a window. No door opened onto the steps. Somewhere below, a loose shutter touched its frame and moved away.

I climbed again.

The cabinet’s control cover had lifted. The black handle would interrupt the feed, release the original and shutter the pen.

The black grip was thumb-worn; the white coating had cracked. I stepped onto the worn mat between them.

The white would complete the live circuit, lift the glass over the writing arm and seal the original away. A plate beside the handles explained the interlock. Once it tripped, the spring held the other face shut.

I looked for a way around it.

I examined the hinges. I put the knife against a screw and found the slot too narrow. I tapped the glass with my ring finger, then harder with a knuckle. It was thick and laminated, set deep into the frame. If I broke it, fragments would fall into the pen mechanism. If I forced the master compartment, I could destroy the seal that made its date worth anything.

The room offered the two handles again.

I laid the papers on the floor because the table had no clear space.

I weighted a curling sheet with the ferry cup. Around it I laid the register pages, radio card, fuel-stained rations, thin ferry lists and chalk-dusted quarry notes. Each brought its place into the room. The garden counts went beside the lantern pictures. The dog lay at the edge, safe from my feet.

I did not put them in the order I had found them.

I put her words together.

I fanned her pages so a little of each remained visible. His upright letters waited by my boot.

At first that gave me the sister I had come to find: feeding children, binding a split handle, keeping heat in a room, leaving a route for me. Reading only her hand felt like coming inside after cold. I could have stayed there. The white handle waited above me.

Then I placed the keeper’s pages alongside them.

The warmth did not vanish. It became difficult to move in. Her kindness remained, but around it appeared the locked doors, the prior orders, the repeated marks. His warnings remained too, now crossed by his own requests and admissions. The papers filled the floor until I could no longer stand without choosing where to put my feet.

I sat among them.

It was fully dark outside.

I tried to imagine completing the circuit. The pen would move. It might write the same message as the one in the forest, repeat the hesitation in the city log, make the backward mark I had trusted. I would have proof that it could. The master would close, taking its date and its earlier instructions out of reach.

I tried to imagine breaking it. The lamps would go out. Somewhere a call might fail. The original would rise, and I might learn whose work had preceded the locks. The pen would remain silent, its ability explained in a manual but not demonstrated to my eyes.

I was frightened of doing something she had asked me not to do.

That, at last, was a fear I recognised without needing to translate it.

I thought of her wet hands at the garden gate. Her impatience while I stood in the doorway. The way she had said *come*, leaving me to decide whether it was an invitation or a rebuke. All my life I had waited for the moment when she would explain herself so plainly that choosing became easy.

The last message offered me a place.

I wanted the person who had made room in a bed to be the person who had written it. I wanted to finish what she could not. Wanting those things made my hand move toward the white handle.

My fingertips rested on the white handle. In the glass I saw myself leaning after them.

I stopped. I lifted my fingers, the index last, and held the hand rigid against my coat.

If she was being held, I would not power the thing that could speak in her place. If she had built a way of speaking without being there, I would have to disappoint her and ask her to speak to me herself.

I gathered the papers. I put the child's dog between two dry pages, tucked the scarf on top, and closed the notebook.

Then I took the black handle.

It was cold and slightly greasy. The first part of its travel was easy. Halfway down, the mechanism resisted. I had to put my other hand over the first.

Rope marks crossed one palm; the landing had scraped the other. I fitted the uninjured skin against the grip and pulled with my shoulders. The spring held.

For a second I thought the resistance was a warning I ought to understand.

I pulled.

The lamps went out.

My reflected face vanished. Downhill, several windows darkened together; a few stayed lit.

A shutter slammed inside the cabinet. A spring released, lifting the original into the faint light from the window. I kept gripping the handle after it reached its stop.

I took the pantry lamp out of my bag.

My hands were too clumsy with the striker. The first spark died. The second found the wick. A small flame steadied, exposing scratches on the lamp’s familiar base. There was still a spot of flour in the seam where my sister had once set it on the kitchen table.

I put it beside the cabinet.

The released panel could be lifted now. Under it lay the original sheet, fixed through its edge to the master plate. Construction lines showed faintly beneath the ink. A day-one seal crossed both paper and metal; its surface broke into tiny ridges around the impression. The locks in the service book were dated three days later.

The writing carried her backward turn in the margin.

I read the first sentence and did not understand it.

*Fit the holding locks only after the master is sealed.*

I read it again.

The lamp flame bent toward the open panel.

*Leave the western route legible. If my sibling reaches the final cabinet, they will break the feed to release me. Their interruption must release this original. Do not let anyone perform it in their place.*

My hands had left damp marks on the edge of the table.

I took them away and held them against my coat.

The words remained.

I moved the lamp closer. No fold hid a word; no stain broke the sentence. My finger followed the line and stopped at *my sibling*.

I braced both palms on the table. My knee struck its underside. The flame shook and the letters doubled.

Before the locks, she had described the decision I would make on this floor, believing I was refusing to be directed. The western route. The interruption. Even my wish to free her had its place in the instruction.

I looked at the black handle.

Its grip bore the marks of my fingers in the grease.

I had imagined that handle as the first thing on the journey that belonged wholly to me.

The original did not look triumphant. There was no taunt, no little flourish to acknowledge that I had arrived. Its lines were compact and practical, crowded into the space beneath a drawing. That ordinary economy made it worse. My fear and anger had been accommodated as simply as the dimensions of a hinge.

I sat down on the floor.

For a while I could hear only the lamp. The wick made a small uneven whisper. Somewhere in the tower, metal cooled.

At the doorway I had said the wrong thing. On the road I had believed arriving could undo it. This older page already contained that arrival.

I wanted to tear it.

The desire was sudden and strong enough that I reached for the sheet. Then I saw the seal, the thin construction lines, the little place where ink had spread along a fibre. I stopped with my fingers above it.

Someone could have made her write it.

The thought came with relief, and I distrusted the relief before the thought had finished. Still, it was possible. A person could anticipate a rescuer under pressure. A person could leave an instruction that served one purpose on its surface and another beneath it. Nothing on the paper showed who stood beside her while she wrote.

But she could no longer be the helpless woman I had carried up the road. In memory, her hands were never folded waiting. Even exhausted beside our mother, they found a seam, a cloth, a knot to work at. Whatever she had suffered here, she had understood more than I had allowed her.

I lifted the lamp.

Below the instruction she had drawn three short strokes and crossed out the third. In its place was a long one.

Two short. One long.

I felt the summer grass against my face so sharply that I turned my head.

Someone is watching.

At the kitchen window, I had waited for the long flash. Here it was, older than my journey, waiting beneath the signal that brought me.

I held the lamp closer. The correction was part of the original. No fresh ink, no loose slip laid on top.

In the low lamplight, pressure marks edged the correction. The long stroke ended in a backward lift. I held the scarf beside it. Thread and ink made the same turn. The warning belonged to the instruction.

I looked toward the window.

The houses down the slope were shapes within shapes. A few lamps still burned. Between them, dark windows reflected pieces of sky. I could see no one at any of them.

In the long grass, she had looked over my shoulder before she laughed.

I had never asked what she saw.

The pen remained behind its shutter. I could hear no movement from it. On the other face lay the answer I had chosen not to witness: the joints, the selector, the machine’s capacity to make her hesitation. It might have copied the warning because she wanted it copied. It might have learned the warning as it learned the rest of her.

I could not make the shut face answer by staring at it.

The passage beyond the room had opened when I pulled the handle. Through it, a stair descended toward the unlit side of the settlement.

I placed the original in my notebook as carefully as I had placed the thread note there in the forest. The seal had released with the sheet, leaving its impression through the margin; I protected it between the stiff covers. Then I rewound my sister’s scarf around my neck.

For the first time since finding it, I noticed the smell of my own skin in the wool.

I went down the far stair carrying the pantry lamp.

## XI. A Door Without a Light

The steps ended in a narrow yard.

Grass had grown between the paving stones. A water barrel stood beneath a spout, and beside it a chair had been turned upside down to keep its seat dry. The wall facing the tower had no windows. On the other side of the yard, a low passage opened into a street I had not seen from above.

I stopped in the passage and put the lamp on the ground.

My legs were shaking. On the road I had kept walking through fear because there was always a next handhold, a door, a rise that would reveal something. Here the stair was behind me and the night ahead. Nothing in the yard required me to move.

I sat beneath the spout.

Water gathered at its broken lip and fell into the barrel.

Drops dented the barrel’s oval of sky. Twice they fell close together, then paused. I began to count, caught myself, and listened to the splash.

The original lay against my chest inside the bag.

I wanted to go back and force the other shutter. I wanted to return to every place on the trail and make the paper give me one more thing: the report missing from the fuse label, the heading washed from the ferry list, the end torn from the quarry note. I could spend the rest of my life arriving just after the part that would explain her.

A light moved on the wall opposite.

I looked up sharply.

It was my own lamp. The flame had bent in a draught, and the shadow of the upturned chair crossed the wall, changing its shape. I watched until it settled. Then I picked the lamp up.

Beyond the passage, the street sloped gently between low houses.

The old paving shone in rounded patches under my lamp. Its gutter wandered with the settled stones. At a break in the houses, a tree stood below a parapet, twigs distinct against sky and lost against the valley. A single leaf held still, edgewise to the light.

Roofs hid most of the tower behind me. Ahead, the street turned beyond my lamp’s reach.

No signal wires crossed this street. At one doorstep lay a pair of small shoes, soles turned outward to dry.

Their laces were loose to the lowest eyelets. Mud remained in the seams, but someone had wiped the uppers clean. A damp rag lay beside a print where one shoe had rested while the other was cleaned.

I crouched, lowered the lamp and touched the leather. Cold, still damp at the seams.

I stayed crouched for a long time.

The door behind the shoes was dark. There was no paper fastened to it, no mark cut into the frame, no lamp that might continue after its maker left. Its paint had peeled around the latch. At shoulder height, a knot in the wood had worked loose and been pressed back from the other side.

I took out the notebook.

Near the beginning, beneath the city map, I found the sentence I had written in the schoolroom: *She had been trying to stay where someone could see her.* I had left a space underneath it.

I looked at the space without filling it.

Then I turned to a clean page near the end. My pencil was short; I had been gripping it too hard. I wrote the date as well as I could reckon it and described the room at the top of the tower. The black handle, the shutter, the seal. I copied her instruction exactly. I did not improve the order of the sentences or leave out the place where I had first misunderstood them.

Below that I wrote what I had done.

*I broke the feed because I thought it would free her.*

I waited until I could read the sentence without reaching to change it.

On the next line I wrote:

*I still want to find her.*

The pencil point broke at the full stop.

I tried to pick up the fragment. My fingers would not close delicately enough; it rolled into the fold. I chased it with a nail and smeared the margin. Then I stopped.

That undid me. I cried without the care I had taken before, my breath rocking the lamp between my boots. I cried for the woman in the doorway, the child in the grass, the six people whose names I knew without faces, the keeper whose fear had not kept his hands off the locks. Most of all I wanted my sister to open this door and be irritated to find me sitting in the wet.

I let the wanting remain that small.

When it passed, I wiped my face on the dry end of the scarf.

Beside my knees, the wet rims of the shoes had begun to dry. I shut the notebook.

There were two shoes on the step. One stood straight. The other had fallen on its side. I set it upright beside its mate.

Then I rose and put my hand against the door.

The wood was cold. Behind it I could hear nothing definite: perhaps the settling of a room, perhaps the water moving in the wall. I did not turn it into a voice.

I knocked without a pattern.

I left my hand near the wood, fingers loose. The lamp reached the shoes, my coat hem, the worn latch. Above them the door rose into darkness.

I waited there, beside the little shoes, for someone to answer.
